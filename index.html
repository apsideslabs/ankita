The two figures the numbers make

A square, and a century.

The Lo Shu square places every non-zero digit of the date — plus the bhāgyāṅka, plus the mūlāṅka when the birth day is ten or more. The daśā divides one hundred and twenty years among nine lords, from the Moon's position at birth.

Questions

Answered plainly.

What is a mūlāṅka, exactly?

The day of your birth reduced to one digit: born on the 29th, 2 + 9 = 11, then 1 + 1 = 2. It is ordinary digital-root arithmetic, and every tradition here agrees on it.

Why is the Lo Shu square called Chinese?

Because it is. The square that sums to fifteen on every line appears in Chinese sources from the fourth century BCE and enters Indian practice late. Calling it Vedic would be a claim the texts do not support.

Where does the digit-to-planet pairing come from?

From the modern Indian literature — Johari's 1990 book states it, including the substitution of Rāhu for Uranus and Ketu for Neptune. No classical Sanskrit verse for it was found, and the page that uses it says so.

Does it predict events?

No. It computes and reads: the numbers, the square, the daśā with dates. It does not print a forecast, because no source in the library supports one.
